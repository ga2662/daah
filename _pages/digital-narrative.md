# Reflection on Digital Literacy

As an 18-year-old university freshman majoring in Interactive Media, I have grown up surrounded by technology for most of my life. Because of this, I used to think being digitally literate simply meant knowing how to use technology well. I know how to navigate social media, search for information online, create digital content, and learn new platforms relatively quickly. However, since starting university and taking this course, I have started to realize that digital literacy is much broader than just knowing how to use a computer or being familiar with the internet. It also involves understanding how digital systems work, questioning the information we see online, working with different forms of data, and being aware of how technology influences the way we understand the world.

In my everyday life, I already use different digital skills without necessarily thinking about them. For content creation, I use platforms such as Canva and social media, where I have learned how to communicate ideas visually depending on the audience or platform. Since starting Interactive Media, I have also begun using p5.js to create projects through code, which is completely different from the digital tools I was already familiar with. For academic work, I regularly use Google Drive, Google Docs, online academic databases, and search engines to find, organize, and share information. I also use spreadsheets occasionally, although I would say I am much more comfortable creating visual content than working with large amounts of data or doing complex data analysis.

One thing I find interesting about digital humanities is that it connects areas that I previously thought were quite separate. In a class with students studying subjects such as Computer Science, Interactive Media, Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Engineering, Literature, and Creative Writing, everyone brings a completely different skill set. For example, someone studying Computer Science might approach a project by thinking about its code or technical structure, while someone studying Literature may focus more on language, interpretation, or historical texts. As an Interactive Media student, I am naturally more interested in how information can become something visual or interactive. Digital humanities creates an opportunity for all of these perspectives to work together.


I have also started thinking differently about computational thinking. Before university, I mostly connected computational thinking with coding, which is something I am still very new to. Since starting Interactive Media, I have learned some basic programming through p5.js, and even that has changed the way I approach problems. Coding forces me to break a larger idea into smaller steps and understand how each part affects the final result. I think collaborating with people from other majors could develop this even further. Someone with more programming experience might approach a problem completely differently from someone with a background in design, writing, or environmental research. Combining those perspectives could create projects that none of us would necessarily be able to make individually.



Data is an area where I still have a lot to learn. I understand basic ways of organizing information using tools such as spreadsheets, but I have very little experience with larger datasets or more advanced methods of data manipulation and analysis. Digital humanities is making me realize that data also does not have to mean only numbers. Texts, images, maps, archives, social media posts, and many other forms of information can become data that we study. I am interested in learning how this information can be analyzed and then transformed into visualizations or interactive experiences.


AI is another major part of digital literacy today. I already use AI for things such as brainstorming, explaining concepts I do not understand, generating ideas, and helping me approach assignments. However, I am also becoming more aware that using AI effectively requires critical thinking. An AI-generated response can sound convincing without necessarily being accurate, so knowing how to check sources and question information is important. I would like to understand more about how AI actually works rather than only knowing how to use it.


Currently, I would say my strengths in digital literacy are creativity, visual communication, and being comfortable learning new digital platforms. My weaknesses are more technical areas such as programming, data manipulation, and understanding what happens behind the interfaces I use. As a freshman, I think this gives me a lot of room to explore. Through this course, I would especially like to learn how creative and computational thinking can work together. Digital humanities gives me the opportunity to look beyond simply using technology and instead think critically about how technology, data, creativity, and people are all connected.
