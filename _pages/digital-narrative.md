# Reflection on Digital Literacy

As a 19 year old university freshman majoring in Interactive Media, I have grown up surrounded by technology for most of my life. Before starting university, I used to think that being digitally literate simply meant knowing how to use technology effectively. I was already familiar with social media, creating digital content, searching for information online, and learning about different platforms. However, since taking Introduction to Digital Arts and Humanities, I have started to realize that digital literacy involves much more than being comfortable with technology. It also involves understanding how digital systems work, questioning the information they produce, and recognizing how technology influences the way we interpret the world. 

When I first completed my Digital Literacy Narrative, I used three different AI models, ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, to generate reflections from the perspective of an Interactive Media student. Although their responses discussed similar ideas, I noticed that some of the experiences they described were not actually mine. For example, Claude mentioned using Adobe Premiere and Python, while Gemini assumed I had experience with SQL. I found it interesting how confidently the models described these experiences without knowing whether they were accurate. Looking back, I realize that this was already an example of why critical digital literacy is important. AI can produce information that sounds convincing, but that does not necessarily make it accurate. 

In my everyday life, I still use many of the digital tools I mentioned in my original reflection. I use platforms such as Canva and social media for content creation, while Google Docs and Google Drive help me organize information and collaborate on academic work. However, university has introduced me to tools I would not have necessarily explored on my own. Through Interactive Media, I have started learning programming with p5.js, which has allowed me to create interactive projects rather than simply using existing platforms. Coding was initially unfamiliar to me, and I sometimes struggled to understand why something was not working. Over time, I have become more comfortable experimenting with solutions and breaking down larger problems into smaller steps. This has helped me develop my computational thinking because I have started approaching problems more systematically. 

One of the biggest changes in my understanding of digital literacy has come from working with data. In my original reflection, I mentioned that data manipulation and analysis were areas where I had very little experience. Since then, I have learned how to use R and explore different methods of analyzing information. For our first assignment, we worked with approximately 1,000 science fiction texts from Project Gutenberg. We used R to examine word frequencies, identify patterns across different categories, and create visualizations. I also learned about TF-IDF, which helped us identify words that were particularly distinctive within certain groups of texts. Before taking this course, I would not have imagined that literature could be studied through computational methods in this way. 

However, something I found particularly interesting was that identifying patterns did not necessarily mean understanding them. For example, a word appearing frequently in a group of science fiction texts might suggest a theme, but we still need to examine how that word is being used within the stories. This reminded me of our discussions about distant reading, particularly Ted Underwood's work. Distant reading allows researchers to identify patterns across large collections of texts that would be difficult to examine individually. However, these patterns still require interpretation. Computers can help us recognize relationships within data, but they cannot automatically explain the cultural or historical meaning behind them. This made me realize that computational analysis and traditional reading can complement each other. 

Another reading that changed how I think about digital literacy was Johanna Drucker's The Digital Humanities Coursebook. I remember revising the chapters for our oral exam and learning about how digital humanities combine computational methods with humanistic interpretation. One of the most important ideas I took away was that data is not necessarily neutral or objective. The information we collect, the categories we create, and the ways we organize it can all influence the conclusions we reach. Before this course, I would usually look at a graph or visualization and assume that it was simply presenting information. However, Drucker made me realize that even creating a visualization involves decisions about what should be included, excluded, or emphasized. 

I also found her discussions of digitization, metadata, and databases interesting because they made me think about what happens when information is transformed into a digital format. Digitization makes information easier to access and analyze, but it can also remove parts of its original context. Similarly, metadata helps us describe and categorize information, but the categories we choose can affect how that information is understood. When preparing for my oral exam, I understood these ideas mainly through the readings, but after working with datasets myself, I have started to understand their importance in practice. 

Our second assignment, which involved spatial data from Lorimer's Gazetteer of the Persian Gulf, Oman and Central Arabia, made me think about these ideas even further. We worked on extracting geographical information from historical descriptions and organizing it into a dataset for mapping. What I found interesting was that transforming historical writing into structured data was not as straightforward as I expected. We had to consider how locations were described and how information should be categorized. This made me question how much information might be lost when a historical document is reduced to individual entries in a dataset. Since the Gazetteer was written from a particular historical perspective, its information cannot necessarily be treated as a complete or neutral representation of the region. This reminded me of Drucker's argument that the way information is collected and structured influences how we understand it. 

Another aspect of digital humanities that I appreciate is how it brings together students from different academic backgrounds. Someone studying Computer Science might focus on how to process data, while someone studying Art and Art History might be more interested in its meaning or historical context. As an Interactive Media student, I am particularly interested in how information can become something visual or interactive. I think collaboration is important because it allows us to consider perspectives, we might not considered beforehand. It has also made me realize that computational thinking is not limited to coding, but involves organizing information, recognizing patterns, and questioning our methods. 

My relationship with AI has also changed throughout the semester. I still use AI to brainstorm ideas, understand unfamiliar concepts, and troubleshoot problems, especially when working with programming or data. However, I have become more aware that AI should not replace my own understanding of the work. During our assignments, I realized how important it was to check whether the information being generated was accurate and whether the results made sense. This connects back to my original Digital Literacy Narrative because the three models generated detailed reflections about experiences, I had never actually had. I think being digitally literate also means knowing when to question a tool instead of assuming that its output is correct. 

Looking back at my original reflection, I can see how much my understanding of digital literacy has developed. I am still more comfortable with creativity and visual communication than with programming or complex data analysis, but I now have practical experience with methods that were unfamiliar to me at the beginning of the semester. More importantly, I have learned that digital literacy is not just about learning how to use technology. It is also about questioning how information is created, organized, and represented. Moving forward, I want to continue developing my technical skills while learning how to combine creative and computational approaches. I think the biggest thing I have learned is that becoming more digitally literate does not necessarily mean knowing how to use more tools, but becoming more thoughtful and critical about the ones I already use.
