# Reflection on Digital Literacy

As an 18-year-old university freshman majoring in Interactive Media, I have grown up surrounded by technology for most of my life. Because of this, I used to think being digitally literate simply meant knowing how to use technology well. I know how to navigate social media, search for information online, create digital content, and learn new platforms relatively quickly. However, since starting university and taking this course, I have started to realize that digital literacy is much broader than just knowing how to use a computer or being familiar with the internet. It also involves understanding how digital systems work, questioning the information we see online, working with different forms of data, and being aware of how technology influences the way we understand the world.

In my everyday life, I already use different digital skills without necessarily thinking about them. For content creation, I use platforms such as Canva and social media, where I have learned how to communicate ideas visually depending on the audience or platform. Since starting Interactive Media, I have also begun using p5.js to create projects through code, which is completely different from the digital tools I was already familiar with. For academic work, I regularly use Google Drive, Google Docs, online academic databases, and search engines to find, organize, and share information. I also use spreadsheets occasionally, although I would say I am much more comfortable creating visual content than working with large amounts of data or doing complex data analysis.

One thing I find interesting about digital humanities is that it connects areas that I previously thought were quite separate. In a class with students studying subjects such as Computer Science, Interactive Media, Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Engineering, Literature, and Creative Writing, everyone brings a completely different skill set. For example, someone studying Computer Science might approach a project by thinking about its code or technical structure, while someone studying Literature may focus more on language, interpretation, or historical texts. As an Interactive Media student, I am naturally more interested in how information can become something visual or interactive. Digital humanities creates an opportunity for all of these perspectives to work together.


I have also started thinking differently about computational thinking. Before university, I mostly connected computational thinking with coding, which is something I am still very new to. Since starting Interactive Media, I have learned some basic programming through p5.js, and even that has changed the way I approach problems. Coding forces me to break a larger idea into smaller steps and understand how each part affects the final result. I think collaborating with people from other majors could develop this even further. Someone with more programming experience might approach a problem completely differently from someone with a background in design, writing, or environmental research. Combining those perspectives could create projects that none of us would necessarily be able to make individually.



Data is an area where I still have a lot to learn. I understand basic ways of organizing information using tools such as spreadsheets, but I have very little experience with larger datasets or more advanced methods of data manipulation and analysis. Digital humanities is making me realize that data also does not have to mean only numbers. Texts, images, maps, archives, social media posts, and many other forms of information can become data that we study. I am interested in learning how this information can be analyzed and then transformed into visualizations or interactive experiences.


AI is another major part of digital literacy today. I already use AI for things such as brainstorming, explaining concepts I do not understand, generating ideas, and helping me approach assignments. However, I am also becoming more aware that using AI effectively requires critical thinking. An AI-generated response can sound convincing without necessarily being accurate, so knowing how to check sources and question information is important. I would like to understand more about how AI actually works rather than only knowing how to use it.


Currently, I would say my strengths in digital literacy are creativity, visual communication, and being comfortable learning new digital platforms. My weaknesses are more technical areas such as programming, data manipulation, and understanding what happens behind the interfaces I use. As a freshman, I think this gives me a lot of room to explore. Through this course, I would especially like to learn how creative and computational thinking can work together. Digital humanities gives me the opportunity to look beyond simply using technology and instead think critically about how technology, data, creativity, and people are all connected.

<h2>Claude</h2>
Coming into university, I assumed "digital literacy" basically meant knowing how to use apps well — being fast on Instagram, comfortable editing a TikTok, able to Google my way out of most problems. A few weeks into this course, I've realized that's a pretty narrow slice of what digital literacy actually means. It's less about fluency with specific platforms and more about understanding how digital systems shape the way I create, communicate, and think — and having enough critical distance to use them intentionally rather than just being used by them.

As an Interactive Media major, a lot of the "strategies" I already use daily turn out to be more sophisticated than I gave myself credit for. For content creation, I move between Adobe Premiere and DaVinci Resolve for video editing, Figma for interface mockups and quick prototyping, and Procreate for illustration work — switching tools depending on whether I need speed, precision, or collaboration features. For social interaction, I'm constantly translating ideas across registers: a project update in a Slack channel with classmates reads very differently from the same update captioned for an Instagram story. I didn't think of that as a "skill" before this class, but it's really a form of audience awareness and rhetorical flexibility that digital literacy frameworks would recognize as core competencies.

On the research and data side, I lean on Zotero for citation management, Notion for organizing project notes and readings, and Google Sheets or occasionally Python with pandas when a dataset gets too unwieldy for spreadsheets alone. None of this felt like "data manipulation and analysis" until I had to explain my workflow to a classmate majoring in Environmental Studies, who uses very similar tools — R and GIS software — but for entirely different ends: tracking coastal erosion data rather than user engagement metrics. That comparison was clarifying. The tools converge even when the disciplines diverge wildly.

That's actually the part of this course I've found most eye-opening: sitting in a room with Computer Science majors who think in terms of algorithms and efficiency, Film and New Media students who think in terms of narrative and affect, Engineering students who think in terms of systems and constraints, and Literature and Creative Writing majors who think in terms of language, ambiguity, and interpretation. We're all technically "digitally literate," but we've each developed a different dialect of that literacy. Digital humanities, as a field, feels like the space where these dialects can actually talk to each other — where a close-reading sensibility from literature can inform how someone visualizes a dataset, or where computational thinking can help a writer map the structure of a nonlinear narrative.

Working in interdisciplinary groups has pushed my own computational thinking further than I expected. I came in thinking computational thinking meant "coding," but I'm learning it's really about decomposition — breaking a fuzzy creative problem into steps a system (or a collaborator) can act on. Explaining my process to someone from a completely different field forces me to articulate assumptions I usually leave implicit, which is uncomfortable but genuinely useful.

Where I feel least confident is in understanding what's actually happening underneath the AI tools I use constantly — image generators, writing assistants, recommendation algorithms. I can operate them fluently, but I don't yet have a strong grasp of the statistical and ethical scaffolding underneath: how training data shapes outputs, where bias enters a pipeline, what "understanding" even means for a model versus a person. That gap feels like the most important one to close, both because these tools are becoming infrastructure for creative work and because Interactive Media sits right at the intersection of designing with these systems and critiquing them.

Going forward, I want to get more comfortable with the technical side of data — not to become a data scientist, but so I can ask sharper questions of the datasets and models I'll increasingly be designing experiences around. I also want to keep working across disciplinary lines, because the moments in this course when I've learned the most weren't the technical demos — they were the conversations where someone from a totally different major looked at my work and asked a question I hadn't thought to ask myself.

<h3>Gemini</h3>
As an 18-year-old first-year Interactive Media major, my relationship with digital technology has always felt second nature, yet entering university has fundamentally altered how I define "digital literacy." Growing up, being digitally literate meant knowing how to navigate social interfaces intuitively, edit videos on capCut, build clean portfolios in Figma, or troubleshoot basic software glitches. In my personal and academic life, content creation and social interaction are seamlessly integrated through strategies like asynchronous collaboration on Notion, rapid prototyping via p5.js, and leveraging platform algorithms across TikTok, Instagram, and Discord to curate distinct personal and creative communities.

However, moving from passive fluency to critical digital literacy has revealed how much deeper the ecosystem runs. Beyond front-end creation, managing and understanding data has become a core element of my workflow. For research data management, I relied on tools like Zotero, Google Drive, and Notion databases. For data manipulation and preliminary analysis, I have begun utilizing platforms like Python (Pandas), Google Sheets, and basic SQL to parse through datasets. Yet, sitting in a classroom filled with peers from diverse interdisciplinary majors—ranging from Computer Science and Engineering to Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Literature, and Creative Writing—has shown me that "literacy" is not a uniform benchmark, but a spectrum of specialized perspectives.

In our shared space, the Computer Science and Engineering students view data through algorithmic efficiency and system architecture. The Literature and Creative Writing majors critique narrative structure, discourse, and bias within digital texts. Environmental Studies students bring spatial data, visualization demands, and real-world ecological metrics to the table, while Film students prioritize temporal aesthetics and audience immersion. As an Interactive Media student, I often find myself acting as a bridge—interested in both technical execution and the human-centered experience.

This interdisciplinary mix highlights the transformative potential of Digital Humanities (DH). Digital Humanities offers a new horizon for my studies by demonstrating that computation is not just a utility to solve technical problems, but a humanistic lens to critique, analyze, and reframe world history, language, and culture. Applying text-mining algorithms to historical literature or visualizing spatial data from socio-environmental studies shows how quantitative methods can serve qualitative inquiry.

Collaborating with peers across these disciplines provides new avenues for expanding my computational thinking. Computational thinking—breaking down complex problems, recognizing patterns, abstracting details, and designing algorithmic solutions—isn't restricted to writing clean code. When a Literature student asks how an algorithm interprets metaphor, or an Environmental Studies student questions how sensory data is mapped visually, it forces me to refactor my assumptions about logic and structure. Collaboration demands that we translate across specialized vocabularies, encouraging a hybrid form of problem-solving where human intuition informs computational logic.

Furthermore, this environment challenges us to critically approach data and the evolving landscape of Artificial Intelligence. Rather than viewing AI models as black boxes or ultimate authorities, we learn to examine the human labor, cultural biases, and dataset omissions that shape them. Treating data not as objective truth, but as a constructed narrative, changes how I interface with machine learning models and automated data analysis tools.

Reflecting critically on my current abilities, I recognize both my strengths and my limitations. I am confident in my UI/UX design intuition, front-end visual creation, and basic scripting abilities. However, this course is exposing significant gaps in my foundational understanding of computational logic, statistical data analysis, and the ethical implications of data collection. I am discovering that knowing how to use a tool is vastly different from understanding why it was built that way and what implicit boundaries it imposes on the user.

Moving forward, I want to explore new ways of thinking that bridge creative expression with critical theory. I aim to go beyond surface-level interactivity to develop algorithmic art, ethical data visualizations, and physical computing projects that challenge how society interacts with technology. By integrating the analytical rigor of the humanities with the systematic approach of computer science, I hope to evolve from a proficient digital consumer into a mindful, critical digital architect.
